# If Communism Failed… Why Did China Get Rich? (Documentary Version)

**Target:** 1,925 words (±5%) · **Final:** 1,907 words · ~10:39 at 2.98 wps

In 1978, the average person in China produced less than the average person in most of sub-Saharan Africa.

Three years later, close to nine in ten Chinese still lived in extreme poverty.

Today, China makes almost a third of everything manufactured on Earth. It runs more high-speed rail than the rest of the world combined. And it has the second-largest economy on the planet.

The party in charge is still called the Communist Party.

So here's the puzzle.

If communism failed everywhere else, from the Soviet Union to East Germany, how did the biggest communist country end up rich?

The short answer is that it didn't follow the rulebook of either side.

It let markets in, one experiment at a time, starting at the edges of the map. And the party kept its grip on everything that mattered.

One village broke the law and changed the whole country. One fishing district became a city of seventeen million. And one decision in the 1990s cost tens of millions of people their jobs.

That mix built the fastest rise in history.

It also built the four problems China is facing right now. And one of them comes down to a single number that worries Beijing more than any other.

We'll get to it at the end.

First, you have to see how bad things were.

In 1958, Mao Zedong launched the Great Leap Forward. Farmers were herded into giant communes, private plots were banned, and villages were ordered to melt down pots and tools in backyard furnaces to hit steel targets.

Officials reported record harvests that didn't exist. The state took grain based on those fake numbers.

By most estimates, tens of millions of people starved to death between 1959 and 1961.

Then came the Cultural Revolution, from 1966 to 1976. Universities closed. Teachers, engineers and managers were attacked or sent to the countryside.

When Mao died in 1976, China was enormous, isolated and desperately poor.

In December 1978, a party meeting in Beijing confirmed a new direction under Deng Xiaoping, a veteran revolutionary who had been purged twice.

Deng didn't publish a grand plan. His approach was later summed up in a phrase: crossing the river by feeling for the stones.

Try something small. If it works, spread it. If it fails, nobody has to admit the whole system was wrong.

Experiment number one: the farm.

In late 1978, in Xiaogang, a poor village in Anhui province, eighteen farmers secretly signed an agreement to split the commune's land into family plots.

Each family would hand the state its quota. Anything above that, they could keep or sell.

It was illegal. They also agreed that if the leaders were jailed, the rest of the village would raise their children.

The next harvest was several times bigger than the year before.

Instead of punishing them, provincial leaders took notice. Within a few years, the household responsibility system had spread across almost the whole country.

At the same time, the state raised the price it paid for grain by around a fifth. Farmers suddenly had a reason to grow more, and a way to profit from it.

Between 1978 and 1984, China's grain output rose by about a third.

Farmers with spare cash and spare time started small rural factories, making bricks, clothes and tools. These township and village enterprises became one of the fastest-growing parts of the economy.

Nobody had privatized anything. The land still belonged to the collective.

But for the first time in decades, working harder meant earning more.

The cities got a similar trick, called dual-track pricing. Factories still had to deliver a fixed quota at state prices. Anything they made above that quota, they could sell at market prices. The plan stayed in place, but the market slowly grew around it.

Experiment number two: the zones.

Now look at the map.

China's south coast, around the mouth of the Pearl River, sits right next to Hong Kong, a British colony full of capital, shipping and factory owners looking for cheap land and labor.

In 1980, China created four Special Economic Zones, all on the southern coast: Shenzhen, Zhuhai, Shantou and Xiamen.

Inside them, the normal rules were switched off. Foreign companies could own factories, pay lower taxes, hire and fire workers, and export what they made.

Shenzhen was the big one. It sat directly across the border from Hong Kong.

In 1980, it was a collection of fishing villages and farmland with a few hundred thousand people.

Today, it's a city of over seventeen million, home to some of China's biggest tech companies.

In the 1980s, one tower there was famous for rising a floor every three days. People called it Shenzhen speed.

The zones worked as a giant laboratory. If an idea succeeded inside the fence, it could be copied outside. If it failed, the damage stayed contained.

In 1984, fourteen more coastal cities were opened up.

Notice the pattern. Everything started on the coast, where the ports were, close to Hong Kong, Taiwan and Japan.

That geography would come back to haunt China later.

Experiment number three: the open door.

Reform wasn't a straight line.

By the late 1980s, inflation had surged, and anger over corruption was growing. In 1989, protests spread to Tiananmen Square in Beijing, and the army crushed them.

For the next three years, hardliners pushed back against reform.

Then, in 1992, Deng, by then in his late eighties and with no official top post, toured the southern zones and publicly praised them.

The message was clear: keep going.

Money flooded in. By the mid-1990s, China had become one of the biggest destinations for foreign investment in the world.

Factories from Hong Kong, Taiwan, Japan, South Korea and the West moved in, bringing machines, designs and know-how.

China supplied something no other country could match: hundreds of millions of workers moving from farms to cities.

In 2001, China joined the World Trade Organization. Its exports grew more than fivefold in seven years.

By 2009, it was the world's largest exporter.

The ports grew with it. Since 2010, Shanghai has been the busiest container port on Earth, and several of the world's ten busiest ports are now Chinese.

But the hardest experiment was still to come: what to do with the companies the state already owned.

Experiment number four: the state companies.

While private and foreign firms boomed, the old state-owned enterprises were drowning in losses.

In the late 1990s, under premier Zhu Rongji, the government took drastic action. Its slogan was "grasp the large, let go of the small."

Thousands of smaller state firms were sold, merged or shut down. Tens of millions of workers lost what had been jobs for life. Old industrial cities in the northeast, once the pride of the planned economy, were hit hardest.

But the biggest companies were kept, and made stronger: banks, oil, power, telecoms, railways, steel.

That's the key difference from the West.

China let the market run shoes, phones and furniture. The party kept the commanding heights: the banks that decide who gets credit, and the companies that control energy and infrastructure.

Which meant that when the 2008 financial crisis hit, Beijing could act faster than almost anyone.

It announced a stimulus of four trillion yuan, and state banks opened the taps.

China built airports, highways, bridges and entire new districts. Its first high-speed line opened in 2008. Today the network runs over forty-five thousand kilometers.

By one widely cited estimate, China used more cement in three years than the United States used in the entire twentieth century.

Since 1980, according to the World Bank, close to eight hundred million people in China have escaped extreme poverty.

No country has ever done anything like it.

But that same machine came with a bill.

Trade-off number one: inequality.

Remember that everything started on the coast?

Shanghai, Shenzhen and the coastal provinces raced ahead, while much of the interior and the west fell behind.

And China's household registration system, called hukou, ties public services like schooling and healthcare to where you're registered, not where you live.

Today, close to three hundred million migrant workers keep the cities running, often without full access to those services.

By the government's own figures, China's inequality is now higher than in most developed countries.

Trade-off number two: debt.

When growth slowed, the answer was usually more lending and more building.

Local governments, barred for years from borrowing directly, set up their own financing companies to fund roads, bridges and new towns.

Some of those projects made sense. Others didn't. In Inner Mongolia, the new district of Kangbashi was built for hundreds of thousands of people, and for years stood almost empty.

By most estimates, China's total debt is now close to three times the size of its economy.

Trade-off number three: property.

For a typical Chinese family, an apartment became the main way to save. By some estimates, around seven in ten yuan of household wealth sits in property.

Real estate and the industries around it grew to roughly a quarter of the economy.

In 2020, Beijing finally tightened the rules on developer borrowing.

The biggest developer, Evergrande, owed more than three hundred billion dollars. In 2024, a Hong Kong court ordered it to be liquidated.

Millions of families had paid for apartments that hadn't been finished. Some stopped paying their mortgages in protest.

And because so much wealth sits in property, when prices fall, families feel poorer and spend less.

Trade-off number four: people.

From 1980, most urban families were limited to one child. The policy wasn't fully scrapped until 2015.

In 2022, China's population fell for the first time in sixty years. The next year, India overtook it as the most populous country on Earth.

Which brings us back to that one number.

In 2016, about eighteen million babies were born in China.

By 2023, it was about nine million.

Half as many, in seven years.

The workforce that built the miracle is now shrinking and getting older, faster than almost anywhere in history. China risks getting old before it gets rich.

And it isn't rich yet, not per person. The average Chinese still produces only about a sixth as much as the average American.

So, the scorecard.

Geography: a long coastline, great river mouths, and Hong Kong right next door. The reforms started exactly where the map was strongest.

Resources: not oil or gold. People. Hundreds of millions of workers leaving the fields at once.

History: the disasters of the Mao era were so severe that even the party accepted change was needed.

Decisions: small experiments first, then copied at enormous scale. Markets for prices and profits. The party kept control of credit, land and the biggest companies.

That control made China fast. It also let debt, empty apartments and bad bets pile up with very few brakes.

So, did communism fail?

As an economic system, much of it was quietly abandoned.

As a political system, the party is still in charge.

China didn't get rich because communism worked. It got rich in the places where the party stepped back, and stayed powerful because it never let go of the rest.

India has nearly the same number of people, a democracy and a younger workforce. So why is it still so far behind?

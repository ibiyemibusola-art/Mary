# THE HOLLOW FILES: ALICE RUGGLES

The Warning He Ignored

A young woman called the police at 12:40 in the morning.

She told them her ex-boyfriend would not leave her alone. He had been to her door late at night. He had left gifts on her bedroom window sill. He had sent a message confirming he had been outside her home.

The call handler asked her a question.

"What do you want to do about it?"

Eleven days later, 24-year-old Alice Ruggles was dead.

The man she had reported was a serving soldier. Police had given him a written warning. He ignored it, drove 120 miles to her flat, and climbed in through a window.

So how did a woman who reported her stalker end up being killed by him less than two weeks later?

This is the story of Alice Ruggles, the warning he ignored, and the family who refused to let her death be forgotten.

Welcome to The Hollow Files.

Alice Ruggles was 24. She lived in a shared flat on Rawling Road in Gateshead, in the north-east of England, with her best friend and colleague, Maxine McGill.

Her parents, Clive Ruggles and Sue Hills, would later describe a daughter who was warm, sociable and independent. She had a job she enjoyed, close friends, and her whole life in front of her.

In early 2016, Alice met a man online.

His name was Trimaan Dhillon, known to friends as Harry. He was a lance corporal in the British Army, a signaller who had served in Afghanistan, and he had been training with the Special Reconnaissance Regiment.

On the surface, he looked disciplined and reliable.

The relationship was brief. But during it, according to the review later carried out into Alice's death, his behaviour became controlling.

In August 2016, Alice discovered he had been contacting other women on social media and dating apps. She ended the relationship.

Her decision should have been the end of it.

Instead, it was the beginning.

When Alice called things off, Dhillon threatened to release intimate photographs of her. The messages and calls kept coming. And then he started turning up.

Dhillon was based at Glencorse Barracks in Penicuik, near Edinburgh. Alice lived more than a hundred miles away.

That distance did not stop him.

He travelled to Gateshead late at night and knocked on her door. On another occasion, he left flowers and a box of Ferrero Rocher chocolates on the outside of her bedroom window sill.

Think about what that means. He knew which window was hers.

Gifts can look harmless on their own. But a gift left at a bedroom window, at night, by a man you have told to leave you alone, is not a gift. It is a message.

It says: I know where you sleep.

Alice recognised that something was wrong.

At 12:40 in the morning on 1 October 2016, she called the police on the non-emergency number, 101.

She explained what was happening. According to accounts of the call, the operator asked her, "What do you want to do about it?"

The review into her death later found that important details she gave, including that Dhillon had been knocking at her door, and a text message confirming he had been outside her home, were not recorded by the call handler.

The next day, 2 October, a police constable visited Alice and took a statement.

Dhillon was then issued with a Police Information Notice, often called a PIN, for harassment.

A PIN is not a charge. It is not a court order. It is a written warning, telling someone that their behaviour has been reported and that if it continues, they could be arrested.

National guidance said a PIN should be served in person by a police officer.

Instead, an officer contacted Dhillon's barracks in Edinburgh and spoke to one of his superiors. Not the military police. Not Police Scotland. The warning reached Dhillon through his chain of command, and he was told to stop contacting Alice or face arrest.

He did not stop.

Alice's flatmate later told the court that Alice felt she had been "palmed off," and that going to the police had been a waste of time.

On 7 October, five days after the PIN, Alice contacted police again. Dhillon had been in touch again, despite the warning.

But the new report was not linked to the earlier one. It was treated as a separate matter.

So the system now held two reports, from the same woman, about the same man, in the same week. And nobody put them together.

Five days later, Dhillon made the 120-mile journey to Gateshead.

On the night of 12 October 2016, he climbed in through a window of Alice's flat.

Alice was alone.

What happened next, I will keep brief. Dhillon attacked Alice with a knife. She did not survive.

Alice Ruggles was 24 years old.

Her best friend Maxine found her in the flat and called 999.

Dhillon was arrested soon afterwards.

At first, he did not deny being there. Instead, he told a different story. He claimed that Alice had attacked him with a carving knife, and that she had been injured accidentally while he tried to disarm her.

It was a story that placed the blame on the woman he had stalked.

In April 2017, Trimaan Dhillon went on trial at Newcastle Crown Court.

The jury heard about the visits, the gifts on the window sill, the messages, and the warning he had ignored. They heard Alice's flatmate describe how frightened Alice had been, and how let down she felt.

On 26 April 2017, the jury found Dhillon guilty of murder.

Judge Paul Sloan QC described the killing as an act of "utter barbarism."

He told Dhillon: "Not a shred of remorse have you shown from first to last."

And he noted that Dhillon had been so focused on getting his story right, he had forgotten even to shed a crocodile tear.

Dhillon was sentenced to life imprisonment, with a minimum term of 22 years. In 2018, the Court of Appeal refused his attempt to have that sentence reduced.

But Alice's parents had questions that a criminal trial could not answer.

Why had their daughter's first call been handled the way it was?

Why had key details she gave not been recorded?

Why was the warning delivered through the army, instead of by a police officer?

And why was her second report not connected to the first?

The Independent Office for Police Conduct, the IOPC, investigated Northumbria Police's handling of Alice's reports.

In September 2018, it found that a police sergeant and a police constable had a case to answer for misconduct, and that another constable's performance had been unsatisfactory.

The outcome was management action and further training.

For the family, that was not the end of it.

Gateshead Community Safety Board carried out a Domestic Homicide Review into Alice's death. At her family's request, it was published using Alice's real name, rather than anonymised.

The review made twenty recommendations, for local services, for national bodies, and for the Home Office and the Ministry of Defence.

One of them was that threatening to release someone's intimate images should itself become a crime.

At the time, that threat, the one Dhillon had made when Alice ended the relationship, was not a specific offence.

In 2021, threatening to share intimate images did become a crime in England and Wales.

Alice's parents did not stop there.

Clive Ruggles and Sue Hills set up the Alice Ruggles Trust, a charity that works to raise awareness of stalking and coercive control, to provide education and training, and to support research.

Since then, they have spoken to police forces, schools and policymakers across the country. They have told Alice's story again and again, so that other people learn to recognise the signs she faced.

Because stalking is rarely a single moment.

It is flowers left on a window sill. A knock at the door at night. A message that says, I was outside. A threat to share private photographs. A warning that gets ignored.

Each one can be explained away on its own. Together, they form a pattern.

Look back at what Alice did.

She ended a relationship that had become controlling.

She recognised that his behaviour was not normal.

She called the police.

She gave a statement.

And when the warning did not work, she called them again.

The responsibility for her murder belongs to Trimaan Dhillon, and to him alone. Alice was not responsible for his choices.

But the people and systems meant to protect her had more than one chance to see the whole picture.

The details of the first call were not fully recorded. The warning was delivered through his barracks instead of in person. And the second report was treated as if the first had never happened.

Nobody can say for certain that doing things differently would have saved Alice. But she asked for help twice in one week, and the second time, nobody connected it to the first.

So, before we close the file, consider this.

Should a police warning for harassment always be delivered in person, especially when the person being warned lives more than a hundred miles away and works inside another institution?

Or should repeat reports about the same person be flagged automatically, so no victim ever has to start from the beginning again?

Alice Ruggles was 24 years old.

She did what she was told to do. She asked for help.

She deserved to be believed the first time.

And she deserved someone watching out for her.

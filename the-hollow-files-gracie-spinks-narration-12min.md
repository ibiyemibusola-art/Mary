# The Hollow Files — Gracie Spinks (12:20 Cut, Narration Only)

A rucksack on a bridle path.

On a footpath in Derbyshire, in May 2021, beside a farm where a young woman kept her horse, someone found a bag. Inside were knives. An axe. A hammer. A packet of Viagra. And a handwritten note with two words on it.

*Don't lie.*

The police looked inside, decided the tools were probably for woodwork, and logged it as lost property.

Six weeks later, the man who owned that bag walked onto the same farm.

A warning before we begin. This is a true story involving stalking and the death of a young woman. Viewer discretion is advised.

Her name was Gracie Spinks. She was 23.

Four months before that bag was found, Gracie had told Derbyshire Police that a man from her workplace had become fixated on her, and that he'd turned up, uninvited, at the stables where she kept her horse.

She named him. Michael Sellers. Thirty-five. Her supervisor.

They graded him low risk.

So by May 2021, the police held two things. A stalking report about a man who kept appearing at her stables. And a bag of weapons found beside those same stables.

Nobody put them in the same room.

Gracie did not die because nobody knew. She died in the gap between people who each knew a part.

Gracie grew up in Old Whittington, on the edge of Chesterfield. Her parents, Richard and Alison, described her as ambitious and friendly. Somebody who made plans and then actually did them.

Her job was lifeguard. Hold on to that. A lifeguard's whole job is watching: spotting the moment someone starts struggling, and getting there first.

And outside of work, there was the horse. She kept it at Blue Lodge Farm in Duckmanton. Morning after morning, the same drive, the same gate, the same field.

She had a routine. Anyone who paid attention could learn it.

In 2020, the pandemic closed the pools, and Gracie was furloughed. In April, she took a job at a warehouse in Chesterfield run by an online retailer called xbite. It was meant to be a stopgap.

Michael Sellers had worked there since 2015. He was a supervisor. The inquest later heard that the two became friendly and met socially a handful of times. Then Gracie made it clear she didn't want it to go further. A brief friendship, the jury foreman would say. Not a romance.

Sellers did not treat her answer as an answer.

Her mother said he became a nuisance at work. The inquest heard a stronger word: obsessed. And it didn't stay at work. He began turning up where she hadn't invited him.

Including the stables.

For him to appear there, he had to know where she kept her horse, and when she'd be there. He had learned the routine.

In February 2021, Gracie reported Michael Sellers to Derbyshire Police for stalking.

That took courage. He was her supervisor. Reporting him put her job on the line. She did it anyway.

Officers spoke to her. They spoke to him. And the case was graded low risk.

Sellers was not arrested. The police watchdog, the IOPC, later listed what was missing. No risk assessment. No safeguarding plan. No check against national police databases. No reason recorded for not arresting him. No record that anyone considered a Stalking Protection Order, a court order that existed for exactly this situation.

A man who wouldn't accept no, who had followed her to the one place she was predictably alone. And a file with almost nothing in it.

Low risk.

Here's what Gracie never knew. In March 2021, weeks after she reported him, Michael Sellers bought knives. His bank statements showed it.

Whatever the police had decided he was, he was busy deciding something else.

In May, a member of the public walking near Blue Lodge Farm found a rucksack on the bridle path, close to the stables, and called the police.

Inside: hunting knives in sheaths. A folding knife. An axe. A hammer. Viagra. And that note. *Don't lie.*

Picture where it was found: on a path beside the field where a young woman kept her horse. A young woman who, three months earlier, had told this same force that a man was turning up at those stables.

The officers thought the tools might be for bushcraft or woodwork. They didn't visit the spot. They took no notes. They didn't even list the contents. The bag was logged as lost property.

The sergeant, a man with thirty-one years' service, later told the inquest he felt like a fool, and wished he could change the past.

Nobody typed "Blue Lodge Farm" into the system. If they had, Gracie's stalking report would have come straight up.

And inside the bag was a receipt. After her death, that receipt linked the rucksack to Michael Sellers. It had been there the whole time.

Then the date. The inquest heard that on the same day the bag was found, Sellers went out and bought more knives.

He didn't stop. He replaced them.

Her father, Richard, later said he found out about the bag on Facebook. "Had we been forewarned," he said, "I would have taken every step to keep her safe."

May turned into June. The bag sat in a property store. And Gracie kept going to the stables, because nobody had told her there was any reason not to.

What comes next is the morning Gracie died. I'll keep it factual, and I won't describe her injuries. If you need to pause, pause.

Friday, the 18th of June, 2021.

Just after half past six that morning, Michael Sellers left his home in Sheffield in his own car. He drove to Rectory Lane, which runs along the fields of Blue Lodge Farm, and parked at the edge of the fields, away from the yard.

Gracie arrived to see to her horse, the way she always did.

Sellers attacked her there. She was found in the paddock and could not be saved. She was 23.

The knives he used, the inquest heard, were the ones he bought on the day his first bag was handed to police.

Officers found his car on Rectory Lane. Late that morning, less than 150 metres away, local people found his body. A coroner later ruled he had taken his own life, and that the evidence, including a note, showed he had killed Gracie first.

There would be no trial. No jury. No sentence. The one man who could have answered for what happened had made sure he never would.

So the questions turned to the police.

Within days, it came out that Gracie had reported him in February. Then the bag came out too. Her mother called it "a complete non-investigation."

Two sets of officers, months apart, each holding one half of the picture. If either half had been taken seriously, the other would have turned up within minutes.

That's the hollow at the centre of this case.

The IOPC found that five officers had a case to answer for misconduct. Two over the stalking report. Three over the bag. None faced gross misconduct. For the two who handled Gracie's report, a misconduct meeting in November 2022 found the allegations not proven.

In November 2023, an inquest jury of ten reached its conclusion, unanimously. Gracie Spinks had been unlawfully killed by Michael Sellers, the man she had reported for stalking.

Derbyshire Police admitted serious failings. But the coroner had told the jurors they could not decide whether those failings contributed to her death.

So the one question everyone in that room was thinking went unanswered. Would Gracie still be alive?

Outside, her father said it in five words. "They just let her down."

The coroner issued a formal warning calling for better stalking training. Gracie's parents now campaign for Gracie's Law: more funding, more powers and better training to deal with stalking. So the next woman who says *he keeps turning up where I am* is heard.

Look back at what Gracie did. She saw the danger early, the way a lifeguard is trained to. She named him. She reported him. She did everything she was told to do.

And every answer was smaller than the problem. Spoken to. Low risk. Lost property. Not proven.

*Don't lie,* said the note in the bag. Nobody lied about what Michael Sellers was. They just never opened the file.

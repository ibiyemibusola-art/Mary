# Who Was Simon of Cyrene? The African Man Forced to Carry Jesus' Cross

The road is narrow. The air is thick with dust and noise. Soldiers shout. Women weep. And somewhere in the middle of it all, a man who has walked hundreds of miles to worship God is about to be grabbed by a Roman hand.

He didn't ask for this. He didn't volunteer. He was simply passing by.

And in a single moment, his name was written into the most important day in human history.

His name was Simon. He came from Cyrene, a city in North Africa. And the Bible says he carried the cross of Jesus.

But who was he really? Why was an African man standing on that road at that exact moment? And why does the Gospel of Mark do something strange, something it does for almost no one else in the crucifixion story? It names his sons.

Alexander and Rufus.

Why would Mark do that? Why would anyone care who this stranger's children were?

Stay with me. Because by the end of this story, you'll see that Simon's few minutes under that cross may have shaped an entire family, an entire church, and the spread of the gospel itself.

This is Ancient Roots Bible. And this is the story of Simon of Cyrene.

To understand Simon, we first have to understand where he came from.

Cyrene sat on the northern coast of Africa, in what we now call Libya. It was not a small village. It was one of the great cities of the ancient Mediterranean world. Built on a high plateau overlooking the sea, it was surrounded by fertile land, rich farms, and busy trade routes that stretched across the desert and the water.

Greek settlers had founded it centuries before Jesus was born. Later, it came under the rule of Egypt's kings, and then under Rome. But Cyrene was never just one people. Native Libyan tribes lived there. Greeks lived there. Egyptians traded there. And by the time of Jesus, a large and well-established Jewish community had lived there for generations.

Ancient historians tell us that Jews had been brought into Cyrene and the surrounding region centuries earlier. They built homes. They raised families. They kept the Sabbath. They studied the Law. They held on to their faith in a land far from Jerusalem.

So when we meet Simon, we are meeting a man shaped by Africa. Born on African soil. Raised under the African sun. Part of a faith community that had made its home on that continent for hundreds of years.

Now, the Bible never describes Simon's appearance. It does not tell us the color of his skin. But it does tell us where he was from. And that detail matters, because it places an African man right in the center of the gospel story. Not on the edges. Not in the background. On the road to Calvary, shoulder to shoulder with the Son of God.

Hold on to that. It becomes even more important later.

So why was Simon in Jerusalem?

The timing gives us the answer. It was Passover.

Passover was one of the three great pilgrimage feasts of Israel. Every year, Jews from across the known world made the long journey to Jerusalem to remember how God had rescued their ancestors from slavery in Egypt.

Think about that for a moment. A Jewish man from Africa, traveling to Jerusalem, to celebrate the night God set His people free from bondage in Africa.

The journey from Cyrene to Jerusalem was not easy. It was roughly eight hundred miles. Some pilgrims traveled by ship, some along coastal roads, some through Egypt. It could take weeks. It cost money. It cost time. It meant leaving work, land, and comfort behind.

People did not make that journey casually. They made it because their faith mattered to them.

And Simon was not alone. The Book of Acts tells us that people from Cyrene were in Jerusalem so often that they had their own synagogue there. A gathering place for Cyrenian Jews, far from home, who still wanted to worship together.

Some believe Simon may have actually lived in or near Jerusalem by then. Mark tells us he was "coming in from the country." That could mean he was arriving from the fields outside the city, or returning from where he was staying during the feast.

Either way, the picture is clear. Simon was a man of faith, far from his birthplace, walking into the holy city at the holiest time of the year.

He had no idea what was waiting for him on that road.

That same morning, inside Jerusalem, Jesus of Nazareth had been through a night no human being should endure.

He had been arrested in the garden. Questioned by the high priest. Mocked. Struck. Brought before Pilate. Sent to Herod. Sent back to Pilate again. And then, He had been scourged.

Roman scourging was brutal. The whip was made of leather straps, often tipped with bone or metal. It tore the skin. It left men weak, bleeding, and sometimes near death before the cross ever touched them.

Then the soldiers placed a crown of thorns on His head. They wrapped Him in a purple robe. They bowed in mockery. They spat on Him.

And finally, they laid the beam of the cross across His torn shoulders and sent Him out to walk.

Condemned men were usually forced to carry the crossbeam themselves. It was part of the humiliation. A public march through the streets, a warning to everyone watching: this is what happens to those who defy Rome.

But somewhere along that road, Jesus' body began to fail.

The Gospels do not tell us exactly what happened. They do not say He fell, though many traditions picture it that way. What they tell us is simple. The soldiers stopped. They looked into the crowd. And they chose a man.

That man was Simon.

Here's what most people miss about this moment. The Bible uses a very specific word to describe what the soldiers did.

Matthew and Mark both say they "compelled" him. In the original Greek, that word carries a legal meaning. It described the right of Roman soldiers to force civilians into service. A soldier could stop you on the road, put his spear on your shoulder, and order you to carry his load. And you had no right to refuse.

Jesus Himself had used that same word earlier in His ministry. In the Sermon on the Mount, He said, "If anyone compels you to go one mile, go with him two."

Now, on the last day of His earthly life, the exact thing Jesus described was happening to someone else, because of Him.

Imagine being Simon in that moment. You've traveled for weeks. You're entering the city for Passover. You're thinking about the feast, about prayer, about family. And suddenly, a Roman soldier grabs you out of the crowd.

You don't know this prisoner. You don't know why He's being executed. All you know is that touching a condemned man's cross, on the eve of Passover, could make you look like you're part of his crime. It could make you unclean before the feast. It could bring shame.

And you have no choice.

Luke adds one more detail that is easy to read past. He says they laid the cross on Simon "that he might bear it after Jesus."

Behind Jesus.

Simon was not just carrying wood. He was walking directly behind the Savior, step by step, following in His footprints, on the road to Golgotha.

Do you remember what Jesus said to His disciples? "If anyone desires to come after Me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow Me."

Where were those disciples now? Peter had denied Him. Most of the others had fled. And in their place, a stranger from Africa was doing, quite literally, what Jesus had told His followers to do.

Taking up the cross. And following Him.

Picture the walk. The heavy, rough wood pressing into his shoulder. The crowd pushing in from both sides. Soldiers barking orders. Women wailing behind them. And just ahead, close enough to touch, a beaten man with blood running down His face, still moving forward toward His death.

Did Jesus speak to Simon? The Bible doesn't say. Did Simon understand who He was carrying the cross for? We aren't told.

But we know one thing. Simon walked that road all the way to the place called Golgotha. The place of the skull. And there, he would have laid the beam down.

And then what?

Did he leave immediately? Did he stay and watch? Did he hear Jesus pray, "Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do"? Did he see the sky turn dark at noon? Did he feel the ground shake when Jesus cried out and gave up His spirit?

Scripture is silent.

But Scripture is not finished with Simon's family.

Remember the detail I asked you to hold on to? This is where it pays off.

When Mark tells the story, he writes that the soldiers compelled "Simon, a Cyrenian, the father of Alexander and Rufus."

Matthew doesn't mention the sons. Luke doesn't mention them. John doesn't mention Simon at all. Only Mark adds those two names.

Why?

Ancient writers didn't waste words. Scrolls were expensive, and every line mattered. Most scholars believe Mark mentions Alexander and Rufus for one simple reason. His readers already knew who they were.

It would be like saying, "the father of Mike and Daniel," to a church where Mike and Daniel sit in the third row every Sunday. You name them because your audience recognizes them.

That means Simon's sons were likely known members of the early Christian community.

And it gets even more remarkable.

Many early traditions say that Mark wrote his Gospel for believers in Rome. Now open the letter Paul wrote to the church in Rome. In chapter sixteen, as Paul greets the people he loves there, he writes these words:

"Greet Rufus, chosen in the Lord, and his mother and mine."

Rufus. In Rome. Chosen in the Lord.

We cannot prove it is the same Rufus. It was a common name. But many Bible scholars have noticed the connection. Mark, writing to Rome, names a Rufus his readers already know. Paul, writing to Rome, greets a Rufus who is well known in the church there.

And look at what Paul says about Rufus's mother. She had been like a mother to Paul himself. Somewhere along his journeys, this woman had cared for the great apostle, fed him, encouraged him, treated him as her own son.

If this is the same family, then think about what that means.

Simon's wife became a spiritual mother to the apostle Paul. And Simon's son became a faithful leader in the church at the heart of the empire.

All of it tracing back to a man who was forced to carry a cross he never asked to carry.

And there may be even more.

Just fifty days after the crucifixion, Jerusalem filled with pilgrims once again, this time for the feast of Pentecost. The Holy Spirit fell on the disciples. Peter stood up and preached. And Acts chapter two lists the nations that heard the gospel that day in their own languages.

Right there in that list are the words, "the parts of Libya around Cyrene."

People from Simon's homeland were standing in the crowd when the church was born. Three thousand people believed and were baptized that day. We don't know if Simon was among them. But men and women from Cyrene heard the message of the risen Christ, and some of them carried it home.

In Acts chapter eleven, after the believers were scattered by persecution, the Bible says that some men "from Cyprus and Cyrene" came to the city of Antioch. And they did something radical. They began preaching the Lord Jesus not only to Jews, but to Greeks. To Gentiles.

And the hand of the Lord was with them. A great number believed.

That church in Antioch became one of the most important churches in history. It was in Antioch that the disciples were first called Christians. And it was from Antioch that Paul was sent out on his missionary journeys.

So who started that move? Men from Cyrene. Africans, carrying the gospel to the nations.

Then in Acts chapter thirteen, we find a list of prophets and teachers leading the church in Antioch. One of them is named Lucius of Cyrene. Another is called Simeon, who was called Niger.

That name, Niger, is a Latin word meaning "black." Some believe this Simeon may have been a man of African descent, known in the church by that nickname. A few have even wondered whether he could be Simon of Cyrene himself, since Simeon and Simon are forms of the same name.

We cannot say that for certain. The Bible doesn't connect them directly. But here is what we can say. When the church at Antioch laid hands on Paul and Barnabas and sent them out to change the world, there were men from Africa standing in that room.

From the road to Calvary to the leadership of the early church, Africans were there.

Not as an afterthought. As part of God's plan from the beginning.

There's one more clue that some find fascinating. In 1941, archaeologists working in the Kidron Valley, just outside Jerusalem, discovered a burial cave. Inside were bone boxes, called ossuaries, used by Jewish families in the first century. Several of the names belonged to people from Cyrene. And one box carried an inscription that reads, "Alexander, son of Simon."

Is this the same Alexander? The same Simon? No one can prove it. But it shows us that families from Cyrene, with these exact names, really did live and die in Jerusalem in that era.

The details of Simon's story fit the real world of the first century.

So what does all of this mean for us?

First, it tells us that God sees the people history overlooks. Simon was not a king. He was not a priest. He was a pilgrim in a crowd. And yet God made sure his name, his hometown, and even his children were preserved in Scripture for two thousand years.

Second, it reminds us that Africa was never outside the story of redemption. From Egypt, where Joseph rose to power, to Cush, where an Ethiopian official would soon carry the gospel home, to Cyrene, where Simon was born, the continent of Africa runs through the pages of the Bible like a thread of gold. When the cross of Christ became too heavy for His human body to bear, it was an African man who lifted it.

And third, Simon's story speaks to every one of us who has ever been pulled into something we never chose.

Maybe you've been handed a burden you didn't ask for. A diagnosis. A loss. A responsibility that landed on your shoulders out of nowhere. Maybe you feel like Simon on that road. Interrupted. Inconvenienced. Forced to carry something heavy while everyone else watches.

But look at what came from Simon's burden.

He thought he was just carrying wood. He was actually walking behind Jesus. He thought it was the worst interruption of his trip. It may have become the turning point of his family's eternity.

The heaviest moment of his life put him closer to Christ than anyone else on that road.

Sometimes the cross you never wanted to carry is the very thing that brings you closest to Him.

Simon came to Jerusalem to remember how God delivered His people long ago. He left having walked beside the very Lamb of God, on the day God delivered the whole world.

He came for Passover. And he met the Passover Lamb.

So the next time you read about the crucifixion, don't skip over his name. Remember Simon of Cyrene. The African man who was passing by. The stranger who carried the cross. The father whose sons and wife may have helped build the early church.

He wasn't looking for Jesus that day.

But Jesus was about to change everything for him.

# The Desert That Feeds the World: How Israel Turned Sand Into Farms

**Target:** 1,925 words (±5%) · **Final:** 1,870 words · ~10:27 at 2.98 wps

More than half of Israel is desert.

In the far south, the city of Eilat gets less rain in a year than London gets in a typical month.

The country has no great river. Its biggest lake is smaller than many reservoirs. And its population has grown more than tenfold since 1948, to around ten million people.

By almost any measure, this should be one of the thirstiest places on Earth.

And yet Israel grows peppers in the desert and sells them to Europe. It farms fish in the Negev. And in recent years, it has had more fresh water than it needs.

And the technology it invented to water a single tree is now used on farms across India, Africa and the Americas.

So how does a country with almost no water end up with a surplus?

The answer isn't one miracle. It's four fixes, each one forced by the failure of the last.

One leaking pipe started a global industry. One city's sewage now waters the desert. And one country turned the sea into its main tap.

And at the end, there's a twist that sums up the whole story: a lake that now has water pumped into it, instead of out.

We'll come back to it.

Start with the map.

Israel is a narrow strip of land, at its widest only about a hundred and thirty kilometers across.

The north is green. Around the Sea of Galilee, winter rain can top seven hundred millimeters a year.

But travel south and the rain fades fast. Past Beersheba, the Negev Desert takes over, covering more than half the country's land.

To the east, the land plunges into the Jordan Valley and the Dead Sea, around four hundred and thirty meters below sea level, the lowest point on dry land anywhere on Earth.

The water and the land are in the wrong places. Most of the rain falls in the north. Most of the empty, flat land is in the south.

There were three main natural sources: the Sea of Galilee in the north, and two underground aquifers, one along the coast and one under the hills.

All three were limited. And all three were shared with neighbors, or under pressure from a fast-growing population.

That pressure arrived fast. After independence in 1948, waves of immigrants arrived, and the population nearly doubled in about three years. Food was so short that the government rationed basics like eggs, meat and sugar for most of the 1950s.

The country's first prime minister, David Ben-Gurion, talked about making the desert bloom. He later moved to a kibbutz in the Negev himself.

But to make a desert bloom, you need water in the desert.

Fix number one: move the water.

In the 1950s, Israel began building one of the largest engineering projects in its history: the National Water Carrier.

It was a system of pipes, open canals, tunnels and pumping stations, designed to lift water out of the Sea of Galilee, which sits about two hundred meters below sea level, and carry it south toward the coast and the desert.

It opened in 1964, running about a hundred and thirty kilometers from the lake toward the center of the country.

For the first time, water from the wet north could reach farms in the dry south.

It was also a source of conflict. The Sea of Galilee is fed by the Jordan River, whose headwaters rise in Lebanon and Syria. In the mid-1960s, Arab states began a plan to divert those headwaters, and the fighting over it became one of the tensions that led to the 1967 war.

But moving water doesn't create more of it. Every drop sent south was a drop the lake lost. And the farms kept asking for more.

So the next question was obvious: how do you grow more with less?

Fix number two: the drip.

In the 1930s, a water engineer named Simcha Blass reportedly noticed something odd near a leaking pipe: one tree was growing much bigger than the trees around it.

The pipe was dripping water slowly, right at its roots.

Most farms at the time watered by flooding fields or spraying them. Much of that water evaporated in the sun or soaked away where no roots could reach it.

Blass spent years developing a way to deliver water drop by drop, through plastic tubes, directly to each plant.

In 1965, he partnered with Kibbutz Hatzerim, a collective farm in the Negev. Hatzerim had been founded in 1946 as one of a handful of outposts in the Negev, on land where little would grow. Together they founded a company called Netafim.

Studies often put drip irrigation at around ninety percent efficiency, compared with roughly half for flooding. Drip can use far less water, and in the right conditions it can also raise yields, because plants get exactly what they need, when they need it.

Fertilizer can be fed through the same tubes.

Desert farms that had seemed impossible started to work.

In the Arava Valley, near the Jordanian border, farmers began growing peppers, tomatoes and melons in winter, when Europe couldn't, and shipping them north.

Israeli scientists also bred long-lasting cherry tomato varieties that helped turn them into a supermarket staple far beyond Israel.

Today, drip irrigation is used on every inhabited continent, and Netafim alone operates in more than a hundred countries. India, with its huge, water-stressed farmland, has become one of its biggest markets.

Farmers even found uses for water nobody wanted. Deep under the Negev lie aquifers of warm, slightly salty water, too salty for most crops. Farms began using it first to raise fish in desert ponds, then sending the nutrient-rich water on to irrigate olives and date palms, which tolerate salt.

But even drip has limits. Farms still needed water from somewhere.

Fix number three: use it twice.

Every city produces a constant stream of wastewater: from showers, sinks, toilets and factories.

Most countries treat it and pour it into rivers or the sea.

Israel decided to treat it and send it back to the fields.

Large plants were built to clean sewage from Tel Aviv and other cities, store it, and pipe it south for irrigation.

The biggest, known as Shafdan, cleans wastewater from the Tel Aviv area, then lets it seep down through coastal sand dunes, which filter it again naturally. It's later pumped back up and sent south to the Negev.

Today, Israel reuses around nine-tenths of its treated wastewater, mostly on farms. That's the highest rate in the world. Spain, which comes second by many counts, reuses only a fraction of that.

In Israel, a farmer's orchard may be watered with what was once a city's bathwater.

But by the early 2000s, even that wasn't enough.

A run of dry winters pushed the Sea of Galilee toward dangerously low levels. The aquifers were being overpumped. In some coastal areas, seawater began seeping into the fresh groundwater.

The government launched public campaigns asking people to save water. Ads showed famous faces cracking like dry earth.

Israel was running out.

The answer came from the one thing it had more of than it could ever use.

Fix number four: make water.

Israel sits on the Mediterranean. The sea is right there, endless and full of salt.

Taking out the salt had always been possible. It was just too expensive and too energy-hungry to do at scale.

Then the technology improved. Reverse osmosis, which forces seawater through very fine membranes at high pressure, became far cheaper and more efficient.

In 2005, a large desalination plant opened at Ashkelon. Others followed along the coast at Palmachim, Hadera and Ashdod.

In 2013, the Sorek plant south of Tel Aviv opened. At the time, it was the largest seawater reverse-osmosis plant on Earth.

Water from Sorek has been reported to cost only around half a dollar per cubic meter. That's a thousand liters for roughly the price of a single bottle of water in a shop.

Today, by most estimates, desalination supplies the majority of Israel's household and municipal water.

At the same time, Israel changed how water was managed. A new national water authority raised prices toward the real cost of supply, cut leaks from the pipes, and made both cities and farms pay for what they used. Israel's losses from leaking city pipes are now among the lowest in the world.

Within roughly a decade, a country that had been running dry had more fresh water than it needed.

And with the region expected to get hotter and drier, more plants are being planned.

Which brings us back to that lake.

For more than fifty years, the National Water Carrier pumped water out of the Sea of Galilee and sent it south.

Now, Israel has begun doing the opposite. Desalinated water from the Mediterranean is being pumped back toward the lake to top it up in dry years.

The lake that once watered the desert is now being watered by the sea.

But here's the honest part.

The title of this video says Israel feeds the world. It doesn't, at least not with food.

Israel imports most of the grain it eats. Agriculture is a small part of its economy, even though farms still use more than half of the country's water, much of it recycled. And many farms rely on foreign seasonal workers.

What it exports is a method: drip systems, water recycling, desalination and farm technology, used on millions of hectares far beyond its borders.

Water here is also deeply political.

The Mountain Aquifer runs largely beneath the West Bank. Palestinians there use far less water per person than Israelis, and control of it remains one of the most disputed issues in the conflict.

The Jordan River, shared by Israel, Jordan and Syria, has been diverted so heavily by all sides that its lower stretch has lost most of its historic flow.

And the Dead Sea, which depends on that river, is dropping by around a meter a year. Thousands of sinkholes have opened along its shores.

Desalination has its own cost. It consumes a lot of energy, and it leaves behind concentrated salty brine that has to be returned to the sea.

Even so, Israel supplies water to Jordan under their peace agreement, and its approach is copied in places from California to Australia. In 2015, an Israeli company helped build the largest desalination plant in the Western Hemisphere, at Carlsbad in California.

So, the scorecard.

Geography: wrong in every way. Rain in the north, land in the south, desert across half the country.

Resources: almost no water, but a long coastline on a sea full of it.

History: a population that grew tenfold, under constant pressure from drought and conflict.

Decisions: move it, drip it, reuse it, make it, and charge what it really costs.

Israel didn't get water because it found more. It got water because it learned to waste almost none.

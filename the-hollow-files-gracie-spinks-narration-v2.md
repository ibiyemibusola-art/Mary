# THE HOLLOW FILES: GRACIE SPINKS

The Warning Signs Nobody Connected

A young woman reported a man for stalking her.

She told police who he was. She explained that he kept appearing where she didn’t want him.

Then, just months later, someone discovered a bag of weapons near the very stables she had warned police about.

Inside were hunting knives, an axe, a hammer, and a handwritten note with two chilling words:

“Don’t lie.”

The police were called. But the bag was treated as lost property.

Six weeks later, 23-year-old Gracie Spinks was dead.

The man she had reported was responsible for her killing.

And when the full story came out, one question became impossible to ignore.

How could the police have Gracie’s stalking report and a bag of weapons found near her stables without connecting the two in time?

This is the story of Gracie Spinks, the warning signs that were missed, and the failures that left her family searching for answers.

Welcome to The Hollow Files.

Gracie Spinks was 23 years old. She lived in Derbyshire, England, and was described by people who knew her as friendly, ambitious, and full of life.

Before the COVID-19 pandemic, she worked as a lifeguard. When swimming pools closed, she was furloughed and needed another job.

In April 2020, she started working at a warehouse in Chesterfield operated by an online retailer called xbite.

There, she met Michael Sellers, a 35-year-old supervisor who had worked at the company for several years.

At first, they became friendly and met socially a handful of times. But Gracie did not want the relationship to go any further.

Her decision should have been enough.

Instead, the attention became unwanted. Evidence later heard at the inquest described Sellers as becoming obsessed with her. The behaviour was no longer limited to the workplace. He began appearing in places where Gracie did not want him.

One of those places was the stable where she kept her horse.

For Gracie, the stables were part of her everyday life. She went there to care for her horse, following a familiar routine.

But when someone repeatedly appears where you are not expecting them, ordinary places can begin to feel unsafe.

Gracie recognised that something was wrong.

And in February 2021, she did something important.

She went to Derbyshire Police and reported Sellers for stalking.

She gave officers his name and explained what had been happening.

Think about that for a moment. Gracie was only 23. Sellers had been her supervisor at work. Reporting him could have made an already difficult situation even more uncomfortable.

But she did not stay silent. She asked for help.

Officers spoke to Gracie and Sellers. Yet the case was assessed as low risk, and Sellers was not arrested.

Later, the Independent Office for Police Conduct, known as the IOPC, investigated the police response.

Its findings identified serious shortcomings. There was no proper risk assessment recorded, no safeguarding plan, no check against national police databases, and no written explanation for the decision not to arrest Sellers. There was also no record that officers had considered applying for a Stalking Protection Order.

These were not minor details. Risk assessments and safeguarding plans help officers understand the danger a person may face and consider what can be done to protect them.

A stalking complaint should not be dismissed simply because the behaviour has not yet become physically violent.

The full pattern matters: unwanted attention, refusal to accept rejection, and repeated appearances at places where someone can be found.

Gracie had reported that pattern.

But the case was graded low risk.

And then, in May 2021, another warning appeared.

On 6 May, a member of the public found a rucksack on a bridle path near Blue Lodge Farm, where Gracie kept her horse.

They contacted the police and handed over the bag.

Inside were large hunting knives, a smaller folding knife, an axe, and a hammer. There was also a packet of Viagra and a handwritten note that read:

“Don’t lie.”

Finding tools does not automatically mean someone intends to commit a crime. But the circumstances surrounding this bag called for careful attention.

It had been found close to the stables where Gracie kept her horse.

And just three months earlier, she had reported a man for stalking her and turning up at those very stables.

That connection should have raised questions.

Could the bag be connected to an existing police report?

According to evidence later heard at the inquest, the officers who dealt with the rucksack thought the items might be used for bushcraft or woodwork.

They did not visit the place where it had been found. They did not properly record the contents. The bag was logged as lost property.

One of the officers involved was a sergeant with 31 years of service. At the inquest, he accepted that he had not given the bag enough attention and said he wished he could change the past.

But there was something else inside the rucksack: a receipt.

After Gracie’s death, that receipt helped link the bag to Michael Sellers.

The connection had been sitting there all along, among the items that had not been properly examined.

The inquest also heard that Sellers had purchased knives in March, after Gracie reported him. He bought more knives on the day the rucksack was found.

Those purchases became important parts of the investigation after Gracie’s death.

Yet at the time, the bag was treated as lost property, and its possible connection to Gracie’s report was not properly explored.

Her father, Richard Spinks, later said he learned about the bag through Facebook.

He said that if the family had been warned, he would have taken every step to keep Gracie safe.

It is a heartbreaking detail.

Gracie had already gone to the police. Her family had no idea that a bag containing weapons had been found near the place she visited regularly.

Nobody warned her about the discovery.

And she had no reason to know what had happened on that footpath.

Six weeks passed.

Then came Friday, 18 June 2021.

That morning, Michael Sellers left his home in Sheffield and drove toward Chesterfield. Evidence shown at the inquest included CCTV footage of him loading items into his car before setting off.

He parked on Rectory Lane, beside the fields around Blue Lodge Farm.

He knew the area. He had been to the stables before.

Gracie arrived that morning to care for her horse, as she had done so many times.

She was following her ordinary routine.

But Sellers attacked her at the farm and stabbed her.

Gracie was found in a paddock shortly afterward. She could not be saved.

She was just 23 years old.

The weapons connected to the attack were linked to knives Sellers had bought on the day the rucksack was found, according to evidence heard at the inquest.

The discovery of the bag had now become an even more disturbing part of the story.

Police searched the area and found Sellers’ car. Later that morning, a man’s body was discovered nearby.

It was Michael Sellers.

A coroner later ruled that Sellers had died by suicide, with the evidence indicating that he had killed Gracie before taking his own life.

That meant there would be no criminal trial of Sellers for Gracie’s murder.

No courtroom where he could be questioned. No jury weighing his account against the evidence. No sentence that might offer her family some form of legal closure.

But the questions about the police response remained.

Why had Gracie’s report been assessed as low risk?

Why were key safeguarding steps missing?

Why had the bag been treated as lost property?

And why had nobody connected the discovery near the stables to the earlier stalking complaint?

The Independent Office for Police Conduct investigated both the stalking report and the handling of the rucksack.

Its investigation identified shortcomings in each.

Five officers had a case to answer for misconduct: two connected to the stalking investigation and three connected to the bag.

However, the IOPC did not find that those officers had a case to answer for gross misconduct, the more serious category that can lead to dismissal.

In November 2022, the two officers who handled Gracie’s stalking report attended a misconduct meeting. The allegations were found not proven.

For Gracie’s parents, these proceedings could never undo the loss of their daughter.

But they could help explain how the situation had been handled and what needed to change.

In autumn 2023, an inquest opened before Assistant Coroner Matthew Kewley and a jury of ten.

For the first time, Gracie’s parents heard evidence about the events leading up to her death, including the police response to her report and the discovery of the rucksack.

They heard how the bag had been found near the stables, how the contents had not been properly recorded, and how a receipt later helped link it to Sellers.

They also heard Derbyshire Police acknowledge serious failings.

But the jury was not permitted to decide whether those police failings had contributed to Gracie’s death.

On 16 November 2023, the jury returned a unanimous conclusion of unlawful killing by Michael Sellers.

The conclusion confirmed that Gracie had been killed by the man she had reported for stalking months earlier.

Outside the inquest, her father expressed his anger and disappointment in five words:

“They just let her down.”

Those words reflected years of grief and unanswered questions.

The inquest could establish what had happened, but it could not tell the family with certainty whether a different police response would have saved Gracie.

That is an important distinction. We cannot know exactly what would have happened if every procedure had been followed. But the failures identified in the investigation were serious, and they deserved attention.

In December 2023, the coroner issued a Prevention of Future Deaths report, highlighting concerns about the police response and the need for better training in stalking investigations.

In January 2024, Derbyshire Constabulary published a report on its response to stalking. Gracie’s parents cautiously welcomed the changes described.

But they did not consider their work finished.

Their campaign became known as Gracie’s Law. It calls for better funding, powers, and training for police and courts dealing with stalking.

Their aim is simple: when someone reports that another person is repeatedly following them, appearing where they are, or refusing to leave them alone, those concerns should be properly assessed.

Because stalking is not always one dramatic incident.

Sometimes it is a pattern. One unwanted appearance, followed by another. A refusal to accept rejection. A person discovering that someone knows where they will be.

When these signs appear together, they deserve serious attention.

Gracie’s case also shows why police records and communication matter.

One set of officers had handled her stalking report. Another set had handled the bag.

The information was not properly connected.

The police did not need to guess who Gracie had reported. She had given them the name.

They did not need to guess which location was important. She had already told them about the stables.

And when the bag was found nearby, its contents and circumstances called for further investigation.

The receipt inside the bag later helped link it to Sellers.

The information existed, but the pieces were not brought together in time.

That is the central failure at the heart of this case.

Not simply that warning signs were missed, but that important information remained separated when it needed to be examined together.

We should be careful not to claim that any single action would certainly have prevented Gracie’s death. Nobody can know that for certain.

But we can recognise that the procedures meant to assess risk, protect people, and investigate suspicious circumstances must be taken seriously.

Gracie did what people are often advised to do.

She recognised that something was wrong.

She reported the behaviour.

She named the man.

She asked the police for help.

The responsibility for her murder belonged to Michael Sellers. Gracie was not responsible for his actions, and she should never be blamed for following her normal routine or continuing to care for her horse.

The purpose of examining this case is to understand what went wrong and make it harder for the same failures to happen again.

So, before we close the file, consider this.

A woman reports stalking. Months later, a bag containing weapons is discovered near a place she specifically mentioned in that report.

Should police systems automatically flag that connection so officers can assess it properly?

Or is the bigger problem the way people make decisions, record information, and follow up on warnings?

Gracie Spinks was 23 years old.

She had a life ahead of her. She cared for her horse, worked hard, and tried to get help when she became frightened.

She deserved to be listened to.

She deserved to be protected.

And she deserved someone watching out for her.

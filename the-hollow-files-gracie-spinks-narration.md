# The Hollow Files — Gracie Spinks (Narration Only)

A rucksack on a bridle path.

That is where this story could have ended. Not in a field at dawn. Not in a courtroom two and a half years later. On a footpath in Derbyshire, in May 2021, beside a farm where a young woman kept her horse.

Inside the rucksack were knives. An axe. A hammer. A packet of Viagra. And a handwritten note with two words on it.

*Don't lie.*

A member of the public found it and handed it to the police. The police looked inside, decided the tools were probably for woodwork, and logged it as lost property.

Six weeks later, the man who owned that bag walked onto the same farm.

A warning before we begin. This is a true story. It involves stalking, violence, and the death of a young woman. Some of it will be difficult to hear. Viewer discretion is strongly advised.

Her name was Gracie Spinks. She was 23.

Four months before that bag was found, Gracie had gone to Derbyshire Police and told them a man from her workplace had become fixated on her. That he had started appearing where she did not want him. That he had turned up, uninvited, at the stables where she looked after her horse.

She named him. Michael Sellers. Thirty-five years old. Her supervisor.

Officers spoke to her. Officers spoke to him. They graded him low risk. He was not arrested. Nobody checked him against national police records. Nobody wrote down a safety plan. Nobody recorded whether a stalking protection order had even been considered.

So by May 2021, the police held two things. A stalking report from a young woman about a man who kept appearing at her stables. And a bag of weapons found beside those same stables.

Nobody put them in the same room.

Now. Let's open the file.

Gracie Spinks grew up in Old Whittington, a village on the northern edge of Chesterfield. Her parents were Richard Spinks and Alison Ward. They described her as ambitious and friendly, and if you read what the people who knew her said about her, that phrase comes up again and again in different words. Driven. Warm. Somebody who made plans and then actually did them.

Her job, before the world shut down in 2020, was lifeguard.

Hold on to that. A lifeguard's whole job is watching. Sitting high above the water, scanning for the moment someone stops swimming and starts struggling, and getting there before anyone else has noticed. Gracie was trained to see danger early and act on it.

And outside of work, there was the horse.

She kept it at Blue Lodge Farm in Duckmanton, a few miles east of Chesterfield. Fields, a bridle path, a stable block. Horses don't care about weekends or weather or how tired you are. They need feeding, mucking out, checking. So Gracie went. Morning after morning, the same drive, the same gate, the same field.

She had a routine. Anyone who paid attention could learn it.

Then, in spring 2020, the pandemic arrived, the pools closed, and Gracie was furloughed. She didn't sit still. In April 2020, she took a job at a warehouse in Chesterfield run by an online retailer called xbite.

It was supposed to be a stopgap. Something to keep her busy and earning until life went back to normal.

It was the first thing in this story she could not have seen coming. It will not be the last.

Michael Sellers had been at xbite since 2015. By the time Gracie arrived, he was a supervisor. He lived in Sheffield. On paper, there was nothing about him that would make a new starter nervous: older, settled in the job, someone with a bit of authority over the floor.

The inquest would later hear that the two of them became friendly. They met socially a handful of times. And then Gracie made it clear she didn't want it to go any further.

The jury foreman, reading out the findings years later, was careful about this. A brief friendship, he said. Not a romance.

That distinction matters, because of what came next. Sellers did not treat her answer as an answer.

Her mother put it simply: he became a nuisance at work. The inquest heard a stronger word. Obsessed. He was her supervisor, so she couldn't avoid him on shift. And it didn't stay on shift. He began showing up where she hadn't invited him.

Including the stables.

Think about what that means. The stables weren't on the way to anywhere. They were her place: the gate, the field, the horse, the hour in the morning that belonged to her. For him to appear there, he had to know where she kept her horse, and roughly when she'd be there.

He had learned the routine.

In February 2021, Gracie went to Derbyshire Police and reported Michael Sellers for stalking.

Pause on what that took. She was 23. He was her supervisor, so reporting him meant possibly blowing up her job. It meant saying out loud that someone she had once been friendly with now frightened her. People in her position often wait far longer, hoping it stops by itself. Gracie didn't wait. She reported it.

Stalking had been its own criminal offence in England and Wales since 2012. Since 2020, police had also been able to apply for Stalking Protection Orders: court orders that can ban someone from contacting a person or going near the places they use. The tools existed. The question was whether anyone would pick them up.

Officers spoke to Gracie. They spoke to Sellers. And then the case was graded.

Low risk.

Sellers was not arrested, and no one recorded why not. Later, the police watchdog, the Independent Office for Police Conduct (IOPC), laid out what was missing from the file, and it's worth hearing as a list:

No risk assessment. No safeguarding plan. No check on Sellers against national police databases. No written rationale for why a named stalking suspect was not arrested. And no record that anyone had considered applying for a Stalking Protection Order.

Put that beside what Gracie actually told them. A man with authority over her at work. Who would not accept no. Who had followed her to the one place outside work where she was predictably alone.

On one side, a pattern that every stalking guidance document tells officers to take seriously: fixation, refusal to accept rejection, turning up at the victim's places. On the other, a file with almost nothing written in it.

Low risk.

It's hard to see what that cost Sellers. He kept his job. He kept his freedom. He had been spoken to, and that was all. For Gracie, it meant she'd gone to the right place, said the right words, and come away with nothing she could hold up between herself and him.

By the time she died, Gracie no longer worked alongside him. But leaving a job doesn't move a stable. The horse was still at Blue Lodge Farm. The gate, the field, the morning hour: all still where he knew they'd be.

Here is a detail the inquest heard that Gracie never knew.

In March 2021, weeks after she reported him, Michael Sellers bought knives.

A detective told the inquest that Sellers' bank statements showed the purchases. The same knives would later be found in a rucksack on a bridle path.

So while Derbyshire Police were filing Gracie's report as low risk, the man in that report was spending money on blades. Nobody was looking at his bank account, of course. There was no reason to. Nobody had opened an investigation that would have led there.

But hold this date in your head: March 2021. One month after the report. Whatever the police had decided Sellers was, he was busy deciding something else.

In May 2021, a member of the public walking near Blue Lodge Farm came across a rucksack on the bridle path, close to the stables. Reports from the inquest give the date as the 6th of May.

They looked inside, and they did what you're supposed to do. They called the police.

Here is what was in the bag, as described at the inquest. Large hunting knives in sheaths. A smaller folding knife. An axe. A hammer. A blister pack of Viagra. And a note that read: *Don't lie.*

Read that list again slowly. Then picture where it was found: on a path running beside the field where a young woman kept her horse. A young woman who, three months earlier, had told this same police force that a man was turning up at those stables uninvited.

The officers who dealt with the bag would later tell the inquest what they thought. Bushcraft, maybe. Woodwork tools. Someone's kit, mislaid.

They did not visit the place where it had been found. They took no notes. They made no recordings. Back at the station, they didn't even list what was in it. The bag was logged as lost property.

The sergeant involved was a man with thirty-one years of service. At the inquest, he accepted that he hadn't given the contents enough attention. He said he felt like a fool. He said he wished he could change the past.

Here is what nobody did. Nobody typed "Blue Lodge Farm" into the police system to see if the location meant anything. If they had, Gracie's February report would have come straight up: a stalking complaint about a man who kept appearing at those exact stables.

There was something else in the bag. A receipt. After Gracie's death, that receipt is what linked the rucksack back to Michael Sellers. It was in the bag the whole time. It was evidence nobody looked for, sitting among the items nobody listed.

And then there's the date. The inquest heard that on the same day the bag was found, Sellers went out and bought more knives.

Think about what that suggests. A man loses a bag of weapons near a woman's stables. He doesn't stop. He replaces them. That same day.

The police had the bag. Sellers had new knives. And Gracie had no idea either thing had happened.

Gracie's father, Richard, would later tell the inquest how he found out about the bag. Not from an officer at his door. From Facebook.

"Had we been forewarned," he said, "I would have taken every step to keep her safe."

Hold that sentence next to the one the police had written. Low risk.

A family can't protect someone from a danger nobody tells them exists. Gracie had done her part. She'd reported him and given police his name. What she couldn't do was see what was sitting in a property store in a police station a few miles away.

May turned into June. The bag stayed where it was. The file stayed closed. And Gracie kept going to the stables, because the horse needed her, and because nobody had told her there was any reason not to.

It was a Friday.

Police later said that just after half past six that morning, Michael Sellers left his home in Sheffield in his own car. CCTV, shown in evidence at the inquest, captured him loading things into it before he set off.

He drove south toward Chesterfield and parked on Rectory Lane, a road that runs alongside the fields of Blue Lodge Farm. Not at the yard entrance, where someone might see the car pull in. At the edge of the fields.

He knew where to go, because he had been there before, uninvited, in the months Gracie had already told the police about.

Gracie arrived at the farm that morning to see to her horse, the way she did. The routine. The gate, the field, the hour that belonged to her.

Michael Sellers attacked her there. He stabbed her.

She was found in the paddock shortly afterwards and could not be saved. Gracie Spinks was 23 years old.

I said I wouldn't say more about that moment, and I won't. I'll say one thing instead. The weapons he used that morning, the inquest heard, were the knives he had bought on the day his first bag was handed to the police.

Police arrived at Blue Lodge Farm and began to work the scene. Very quickly, a name surfaced, because Gracie had made sure there would be one. Officers found Sellers' car on Rectory Lane. A search of the surrounding land began.

At around eleven that morning, local people searching nearby found a body less than 150 metres from where Gracie had been found. It was Michael Sellers. He was pronounced dead just after midday. A black holdall was found near him. So was a note.

A coroner would later rule that he died by suicide, and that the evidence, including that note, pointed to him having killed Gracie before taking his own life.

There would be no arrest. No police interview where his story slowly bent to fit the evidence. No trial. No jury looking him in the eye. No judge reading out a sentence.

The one man who could have answered for what happened to Gracie had made sure he never would.

So whatever accountability was left now had to come from somewhere else. And everyone in Derbyshire Police who had touched this case knew exactly where.

Within days, it came out that Gracie had reported Sellers back in February.

Derbyshire Police referred itself to the IOPC. That's standard when someone dies after recent police contact. But this referral was about to become far more uncomfortable than standard.

Because the bag came out too.

Somewhere in a property store sat a rucksack with knives, an axe, a hammer, Viagra and a note that said *Don't lie*, found beside the stables six weeks earlier. And inside it, a receipt that, when someone finally looked properly, led back to Michael Sellers.

The word that kept coming up from Gracie's family was "failed." Her mother, Alison, later put it more bluntly. "It was a complete non-investigation," she said. "They didn't look into anything."

Lay the two halves side by side. In February, a stalking report graded low risk, with no database check. In May, a bag of weapons beside the same stables, with no scene visit, no notes and no list of contents. Two different sets of officers, months apart, each holding one half of a picture. If either half had been taken seriously, the other would have turned up within minutes.

That's the hollow at the centre of this case. Not one dramatic mistake, but two quiet ones, made by people who each assumed there was nothing to find.

The IOPC investigation ran through 2021 and into 2022.

Misconduct notices went out. First two officers over the stalking investigation. Then three more over the bag: a sergeant, initially on a gross misconduct notice, and two constables.

When the watchdog published its conclusions, it found that five officers had a case to answer for misconduct. Two for how Gracie's stalking report was handled. Three for what was, and wasn't, done with the bag. None was found to have a case to answer for gross misconduct, the level that can end a police career.

Derbyshire Constabulary accepted the IOPC's learning recommendations: training, guidance, how stalking reports are assessed.

Then came the meetings. For the two officers who'd handled Gracie's February report, a misconduct meeting was held in November 2022, chaired by a senior officer brought in from another force.

The allegations were found not proven.

So, laid out plainly: a young woman reported a stalker. Officers recorded no risk assessment, no safeguarding plan, no database check and no reason for not arresting him. That young woman was later killed by the man she named. And the formal finding on the officers who handled her report was: not proven.

For Gracie's parents, the inquest was the closest thing they would ever get to a trial.

It opened in autumn 2023, more than two years after her death, before Assistant Coroner Matthew Kewley and a jury of ten.

For the first time, Richard and Alison sat in a room and heard officers explain themselves under oath.

They heard the detective describe Sellers' bank statements: knives in March, more knives on the day the bag was found. They heard the sergeant with thirty-one years' service say he'd thought the tools were for chopping wood, and that he felt a fool. They heard how the bag went into the system as lost property while the receipt sat inside it, unread.

And they heard Derbyshire Police admit, formally, in front of a jury, that there had been serious failings.

But there was a limit, and it was set before the jury ever retired. The coroner directed the jurors that they must return a conclusion of unlawful killing by Michael Sellers. And he told them they were not to decide whether the admitted police failings had contributed to Gracie's death.

On the 16th of November 2023, the jury returned its conclusion, unanimously. Gracie Spinks had been unlawfully killed by Michael Sellers, the man she had reported to police for stalking months before.

In the record of inquest, the jurors set out the failings. Officers had classed Sellers as low risk. They'd kept minimal records. In many cases, they'd failed to take notes at all, or to keep crucial video footage.

What the jury could not say, because it wasn't allowed to, was the thing every person in that room was thinking. Would Gracie still be alive if any of it had been done properly?

Outside, her father said it in five words. "They just let her down."

Derbyshire Police said it fully accepted the jury's findings and had made what it called "significant" changes.

The coroner wasn't finished. In December 2023, he issued a Prevention of Future Deaths report, a formal warning sent to the people with the power to stop it happening again. It recorded that Derbyshire Constabulary accepted there had been serious failings in how Gracie's complaint was investigated, and that more training on stalking investigations was needed.

In January 2024, the force published its own report on its response to stalking. Gracie's parents had spent two and a half years being told, in effect, that nothing could have been done. This time, they cautiously welcomed what they read.

"The winds of change," they said in a joint statement, "have finally begun to blow through policing."

But Alison was clear that a report wasn't the end of it. They weren't going to draw a line under this, she said, just because a document had been published.

Because by then, the family were fighting for something bigger than one force.

They call it Gracie's Law: a campaign for more funding, more powers and better training for police and the courts in dealing with stalking. The aim is that the next young woman who walks into a station and says *he keeps turning up where I am* gets an officer who knows what that sentence can lead to.

Go back over Gracie's part in this.

She saw the danger early, the way a lifeguard is trained to. She named the man. She went to the police while she was still working under him, knowing what it could cost her. She told them about the stables. She did exactly what every leaflet and every campaign tells women to do.

And every answer was smaller than the problem. Spoken to. Low risk. Lost property. Not proven.

Everything needed to change the ending was already in police hands before 18 June. Her report. His name. The location. A bag of weapons on her bridle path. A receipt with his details inside it.

Nobody had to work out who Michael Sellers was. Gracie had already done that for them. All anyone had to do was look.

Two words were written on that note in the rucksack. *Don't lie.* We'll never know exactly who they were meant for or what he meant by them. But for this case, I'll offer a different reading. The truth was there in February. It was there in May, in a bag on a footpath, with a receipt in the bottom.

Nobody lied about what Michael Sellers was. They just never opened the file.

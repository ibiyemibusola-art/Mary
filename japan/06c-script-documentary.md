# Why Did Japan Get Rich With No Oil, No Land and No Resources? (Documentary Version)

**Target:** 1,925 words (±5%) · **Final:** 1,908 words · ~10:40 at 2.98 wps

At the end of the Second World War, Japan had lost an empire, about three million of its people and roughly a quarter of everything the country owned.

It had no oil. Almost no iron. Barely an eighth of its land could grow food.

Twenty-three years later, it had the second-largest economy outside the Soviet bloc.

No country had ever climbed that far, that fast, with that little.

The usual explanations are hard work, or American money. Both played a part. Neither comes close to explaining it.

The real answer is stranger. Over four hundred years, Japan was hit by five shocks, each one bad enough to sink a country.

Every time, it lost something. And every time, what it lost pushed it toward the thing that would make it rich.

The fourth shock should have ended Japan for good.

The fifth one made it the biggest car producer on Earth.

To understand any of it, you have to start with the map.

Japan is a chain of volcanic islands stretching about three thousand kilometers, roughly the distance from Montreal to Miami.

About three-quarters of it is mountains. The flat land that's left is squeezed into narrow coastal plains, and that's where nearly everyone lives.

The rivers are short and violent, falling straight out of the mountains into the sea. No Rhine, no Mississippi. No natural highway into the interior.

And underneath it all, four tectonic plates are grinding together. Japan takes roughly a fifth of the world's strongest earthquakes.

By almost any measure, this is a bad place to build a rich country.

But the map hides one advantage, and it will matter more than anything else in this story.

Hold on to it. We'll come back to it.

Shock number one: the closed door.

For a brief moment, Japan actually was rich in resources.

About five hundred years ago, miners found enormous silver deposits on the main island, most famously at Iwami. By some estimates, within a few decades Japan was producing around a third of all the silver in the world.

Portuguese, Dutch and Chinese ships crowded into its ports to buy it, much of it bound for Ming China, which collected its taxes in silver.

Then the shogun, afraid of foreign influence and Christian missionaries, closed the country.

Almost all foreign trade was banned. Only Dutch and Chinese merchants were allowed in, through the single port of Nagasaki. The Dutch were confined to a tiny artificial island in the harbor called Dejima.

The silver kept draining out until the mines thinned and the government had to cap exports.

For more than two centuries, Japan sealed itself off.

And when the silver ran out, it had nothing left to sell.

Shock number two: the black ships.

One summer morning, four American warships under Commodore Matthew Perry anchored near the entrance to what is now Tokyo Bay. Two were steam-powered, trailing black smoke and moving against the wind.

Perry delivered a letter and promised to return. He came back the next year with more ships.

Within five years, Japan had been forced into treaties with the United States, Britain, France, Russia and the Netherlands. Foreigners living in Japan were tried in their own courts, not Japan's. And Japan lost the right to set its own import tariffs.

It wouldn't fully win that right back for more than half a century.

Across the sea, Japan's leaders had watched Britain defeat China in the Opium Wars. They knew what usually came next.

Fifteen years after the black ships arrived, a group of young samurai overthrew the shogunate and restored power to the emperor. It's known as the Meiji Restoration.

What they did next had almost no precedent.

Soon after, around half of the new government's top leadership left the country on a single diplomatic mission. More than a hundred officials and students spent almost two years touring the United States and Europe, studying factories, shipyards, banks, schools and parliaments.

They weren't looking for resources. They were looking for methods.

The changes came fast. Within a single year, Japan opened its first railway between Tokyo and Yokohama, ordered schooling for every child, and opened a state-run silk mill at Tomioka, fitted with French machinery.

The mill shows the logic of the whole plan. Japan had no iron or oil to export. It had mulberry trees, silkworms and cheap, skilled labor.

And at that moment, a disease called pébrine was wiping out silkworms across France and Italy.

Within a few decades, Japan had overtaken China as the world's largest exporter of raw silk.

The money from silk, much of it spun by young women working long hours for very low pay, paid for the railways, the machines and the navy.

Then that navy destroyed a Russian fleet at Tsushima. It was the first time in the modern era that an Asian power had defeated a European one.

Japan had become a modern industrial power in a single lifetime. But the same shortages that drove that success were about to drive it somewhere much darker.

Shock number three: the oil embargo.

A country that imports everything always has a choice. It can build what it needs. Or it can take it.

Over the next few decades, Japan increasingly chose to take. It seized Taiwan, annexed Korea and occupied Manchuria, all rich in coal, iron, rice or land.

But it still had no real source of oil.

By then, roughly four-fifths of Japan's oil came from a single country: the United States.

Then Japan moved troops into southern French Indochina. Washington froze Japanese assets, and within weeks, American oil shipments had stopped. Britain and the Dutch East Indies followed.

Japan's navy estimated it had fuel for about two years.

Its leadership faced two options: pull back from China, or seize the oil fields of the Dutch East Indies, which meant war with the United States.

Four months later, Japan attacked Pearl Harbor.

Shock number four: total defeat.

This is the one that should have ended Japan.

American firebombing burned out large parts of more than sixty cities. A single raid on Tokyo killed around a hundred thousand people in one night. Then came Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

When Japan surrendered, the empire and every overseas mine it had grabbed were gone. Industrial output had collapsed. By most estimates, it was under a third of its prewar level. Millions of soldiers and settlers were coming home to a country that could barely feed them.

Japan was back where it had started before the black ships: steep islands, with nothing to dig.

But the world around it had changed, and three things turned in its favor.

First, the American occupation broke up the old landlord system. Over the next few years, the government bought land from absentee landlords and sold it to the farmers who worked it. Tenant farming went from almost half of all farmland to around a tenth.

Second, the yen was fixed at three hundred and sixty to the dollar, a rate that would make Japanese exports cheap for over twenty years.

Third, war broke out next door in Korea. The American military needed trucks, uniforms and repairs, close to the front, and Japan was the closest industrial country. Toyota, which had nearly gone bankrupt months earlier, was suddenly flooded with orders.

And now the advantage hiding on the map finally comes into play.

No point in Japan is much more than a hundred and fifty kilometers from the sea.

After the war, shipping became cheaper than ever. And a country with no mines of its own wasn't stuck with bad ore or distant coalfields. It could buy from the cheapest mines on Earth.

So Japan built its steel mills right on the coast, often on land reclaimed from the sea. Iron ore from Australia and coal from abroad came off giant ships almost straight into the blast furnaces.

Many American steel mills, by comparison, sat inland near old coalfields, with older equipment and long rail journeys.

Barely a decade after defeat, Japan was building more ships than any other country.

Inside Toyota, an engineer named Ohno Taiichi was solving a different shortage: no space and no money for giant warehouses of parts. His answer, later called just-in-time, delivered each part only when it was needed. Factories around the world would spend decades trying to copy it.

Next, the government launched a plan to double national income in ten years. It took about seven.

Then the world's first high-speed rail line opened, days before the Tokyo Olympics.

And then Japan passed West Germany.

Twenty-three years after defeat, Japan had rebuilt almost everything. Except the one weakness it could never fix: it still had no oil.

Shock number five: the oil crisis.

Arab oil producers cut supplies and oil prices roughly quadrupled.

Japan got around three-quarters of its energy from oil, almost all of it imported. The following year, its economy shrank for the first time since the war.

The response was a national obsession with efficiency. Industry cut energy use. Carmakers focused on small, light, fuel-efficient engines. Honda developed an engine that passed America's tough new emissions rules without a catalytic converter, something Detroit had said was close to impossible.

Six years later, oil prices spiked again. American drivers sat in long lines at gas stations and started buying exactly the kind of cars Japan was best at building.

Soon after, Japan had become the world's largest car producer.

By one estimate, five years after the war the average Japanese produced about a fifth as much as the average American. Forty years later, it was more than four-fifths.

But there's a sixth shock in this story, and Japan caused this one itself.

Then an international deal called the Plaza Accord pushed the yen sharply higher. To cushion the blow, Japan cut interest rates, and cheap money poured into stocks and land.

With so little flat ground, property prices exploded. By the end of the decade, most of the world's ten biggest banks were Japanese. At the peak, the land under the Imperial Palace in Tokyo was said to be worth more than all the real estate in California.

The Tokyo stock market peaked on the last trading day of the decade. It didn't close above that level again for thirty-four years.

Banks hid bad loans for years, and the economy stagnated through what became known as the lost decades. Today, the average Japanese earns noticeably less than the average American, and the population is shrinking.

And yet, Japan still imports almost nine-tenths of its energy, and remains one of the richest large countries on Earth.

So, look back at the five shocks.

First, it lost its silver.

Then it lost control of its own trade.

Then it lost its oil.

Then it lost everything.

Then it lost cheap energy.

Each time, the answer was the same: buy raw materials from wherever they were cheapest, ship them to the coast, and add skill, schooling and precision until they were worth far more.

The coast made that possible. The shortages made it necessary.

Japan didn't get rich because it found something in the ground. It got rich because, again and again, it was left with nothing else to sell.

South Korea copied that model, starting from even less, and ran it even faster.

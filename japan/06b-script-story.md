# Why Did Japan Get Rich With No Oil, No Land and No Resources? (Story Version)

If you pull a five-thousand-yen note out of a wallet in Japan today, there's a woman on it.

Her name is Tsuda Umeko.

And the story of how Japan got rich starts with her at six years old, standing on the deck of a steamship, about to leave home for eleven years.

It's December 1871, in Yokohama harbor.

Umeko is the youngest of five girls the government is sending to America. Her father signed her up. Nobody asked her.

She's carrying a doll, and she doesn't speak a word of English.

So why would a country send its children to the other side of the world?

Because it had just realized it had almost nothing else to send.

Look at what Umeko was leaving behind.

A chain of steep islands, about three-quarters mountains. Barely an eighth of the land can grow food. No oil. Hardly any iron. And earthquakes that can level a city in an afternoon.

Even the rivers were against it. They drop out of the mountains so steeply that a Dutch engineer hired to tame them supposedly said they weren't rivers at all. They were waterfalls.

Eleven years before she was born, four American warships had steamed into the bay near Tokyo, pouring black smoke, moving against the wind.

Japan had nothing that could stop them.

Within a few years it had signed treaties so one-sided that it couldn't even set its own import taxes.

So in 1871, Japan's new leaders did something desperate. About half of them got on the same ship as Umeko, and went to see how the rich countries actually worked.

They'd be gone almost two years.

Umeko would be gone much longer.

When the ship reached San Francisco in January 1872, crowds lined the streets to stare at the visitors. Then came the train across the continent, through snow in the Rockies, all the way to the east coast.

For a six-year-old, it must have felt like falling off the edge of the map.

She was taken in by a couple in Washington, D.C. She went to school there, learned Latin and piano, and slowly started forgetting Japanese. At eight, by her own request, she was baptized a Christian.

Meanwhile, back home, another girl was about to leave her family too.

Her name was Wada Ei. She was fifteen, the daughter of a former samurai in the mountains of central Japan.

In 1873, she went to work at a brand-new silk mill in a town called Tomioka.

At the time, rumors were going around the countryside that the foreigners running the mill drank the workers' blood. It was actually red wine. But families were scared, and the mill couldn't find enough girls.

So Ei's father sent his own daughter, to show the neighbors it was safe.

A generation earlier, her family had lived off a samurai's stipend. Now the one earning was a teenage girl.

Inside, she found something she'd never seen: rows of French steam-powered reeling machines, hundreds of girls, and a bell that rang at the start of every shift.

The girls were ranked by skill and paid by grade. Ei worked hard enough to rise quickly. She later wrote about it in a memoir, and you can feel the pride in it: a girl from the hills, running a machine from France.

This is where Japan's first fortune came from.

Not from a mine. From hands.

At just that moment, a disease was killing silkworms across Europe, and the world needed silk. Japan had mulberry trees, silkworms and millions of patient workers.

By the early 1900s, it was selling more raw silk than any country on Earth.

Ei later went home and taught other girls to run the machines. Most of the women who did this work were paid little and worked brutally long hours. Their thread helped pay for Japan's railways, its factories, and its warships.

In 1882, Umeko finally came home. She was seventeen. She had to relearn her own language.

And the country she came back to was changing fast. It had railways now, schools in almost every village, and a growing army and navy.

But a country with nothing has two ways to get what it needs.

It can build. Or it can take.

One man saw clearly where the second road led.

His name was Yamamoto Isoroku. As a young naval officer, he studied at Harvard, then travelled around America. He visited the oil fields of Texas. He saw the car plants of Detroit.

He came home knowing exactly how much America had, and how little Japan did.

He was also a gambler. He loved poker and bridge, and he was good at both. He knew what a losing hand looked like.

By then Japan was taking what it lacked: Taiwan, then Korea, then Manchuria. Coal, iron, rice, land.

But it still couldn't find oil. By 1941, around four-fifths of its oil came from one supplier: the United States.

That summer, America cut it off.

Yamamoto was now the admiral in charge of the fleet. He reportedly told his government he could run wild for six months, maybe a year. After that, he couldn't promise anything.

And yet the plan to strike Pearl Harbor was his.

He didn't live to see the end. In 1943, American fighters shot down his plane over the Pacific.

By 1945, the empire was gone.

Around three million Japanese were dead, and many millions more across Asia. Cities were burned to the ground. In a single night in March 1945, firebombs killed around a hundred thousand people in Tokyo. Roughly a quarter of the country's wealth had been destroyed.

Japan was back where Umeko had started: a mountainous chain of islands, with nothing to dig.

In the rubble near the city of Hamamatsu, there was a man named Honda Soichiro.

He was the son of a village blacksmith. During the war, he'd built a factory making piston rings. American bombers hit it. Then, in January 1945, an earthquake finished it off.

He sold what was left and, for about a year, did almost nothing. By his own account, he made homemade liquor and played the shakuhachi flute.

Then he noticed something.

People couldn't get gasoline. They couldn't get buses or trains that ran on time. But they had bicycles.

And the army had left behind hundreds of small engines, built to power its radios.

So Honda clipped them onto bicycles.

There wasn't even enough gasoline to run them. So the first ones often ran on a fuel distilled from pine roots. They smoked so much, people joked they were riding chimneys.

They sold as fast as he could make them. When the army engines ran out, he designed his own.

In 1958, his company released a small step-through motorbike called the Super Cub. It went on to become the most-produced motor vehicle in history, more than a hundred million of them.

That little workshop became Honda.

A few hours down the coast, another company was about to collapse.

In 1950, Toyota had cars it couldn't sell and loans it couldn't repay. The founder's son, Toyoda Kiichiro, agreed to lay off over a thousand workers. Then he resigned himself, to take responsibility.

He died two years later and never saw what came next.

That same year, war broke out next door in Korea, and the American military started ordering trucks. Lots of them. The company that had nearly gone under was suddenly struggling to keep up.

A production engineer named Ohno Taiichi, who had started out at the Toyoda family's textile company, was trying to solve a simple problem. Toyota had no room for giant warehouses like Detroit's, and no money to fill them.

The answer came from an unlikely place: the American supermarket.

A shop only puts more milk on the shelf after someone buys the last carton. So Ohno made his factory work the same way. Every part arrived just as it was needed. Nothing sat around.

He called it just-in-time.

And any worker on the line could pull a cord and stop everything the moment they saw a mistake.

Plenty of people inside Toyota hated the idea at first. Within a few decades, factories all over the world were copying him.

Through the 1950s and 1960s, Japan grew about ten percent a year. Ships, steel, televisions, cars.

In 1964, Tokyo hosted the Olympics, and the world's fastest train opened just days before. Less than twenty years earlier, much of that city had been ash.

Then, in 1973, the old weakness came back to bite.

Arab oil producers cut supplies, and oil prices roughly quadrupled. Japan imported almost all of its oil. The next year, its economy shrank for the first time since the war.

But remember Honda?

He'd spent his life making the most of very little fuel.

His engineers had just built an engine clean enough to meet America's tough new pollution rules without a catalytic converter. Detroit had said it couldn't be done.

So when Americans found themselves waiting in long lines at gas stations, there was a small, cheap Honda waiting for them.

By 1980, Japan made more cars than any other country on Earth.

By one estimate, in 1950 the average Japanese produced about a fifth as much as the average American. By 1990, more than four-fifths.

Now, here's the honest part.

It didn't all end well.

In the late 1980s, Japan had so much money and so little flat land that property prices went mad. At the peak, the ground under the Imperial Palace in Tokyo was said to be worth more than all of California. Then the bubble burst. The stock market took thirty-four years to get back to its peak.

Today, the average Japanese earns noticeably less than the average American. And the country is getting older.

But it's still one of the richest large nations on Earth.

So let's go back to the woman on the money.

When Umeko came home, she found that almost nobody in Japan thought girls needed a real education.

So in 1900, with ten students, she opened her own school for women.

It's still running today.

She never married. She spent the rest of her life teaching, and when she died in 1929, the school she'd started in a rented house was on its way to becoming a university.

And almost a century later, Japan put her face on its money.

Think about everyone in this story.

A six-year-old sent across an ocean. A teenage girl at a silk mill. An admiral who knew the odds. A blacksmith's son with a bicycle. An engineer who learned from a grocery shelf.

None of them had oil. None of them had land.

Every one of them was working with less than they needed.

And that's how Japan got rich. Not by finding something in the ground, but by betting everything on the people standing on it.

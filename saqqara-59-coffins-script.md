# They Found 59 Sealed Coffins Beneath Egypt — Who Are They?

**Target length:** 1,600 words (acceptable range 1,520–1,680)

---

## 1. HOOK

In 2020, archaeologists dropped down a narrow shaft in the Egyptian desert. Thirty-something feet below the sand, their flashlights hit wood. Painted wood. Coffins. Stacked one on top of another like someone had packed a moving van and then never came back.

And here's the part that feels wrong. They were sealed. Untouched. For about 2,500 years. In one of the most looted graveyards on Earth.

By the end of the digging, there were fifty-nine of them.

So who was inside? Why were they all crammed together? And how on Earth did tomb robbers miss them?

## 2. SETTING

To answer that, we need to talk about where they were found. Saqqara.

Saqqara sits about twenty miles south of modern Cairo. It was the main cemetery for Memphis, which was Egypt's capital for a really long time. If Memphis was the city, Saqqara was its eternal suburb. People were buried here for something like three thousand years. It's home to the Step Pyramid of Djoser, the oldest big stone pyramid in the world. So this place isn't some hidden corner. It's basically ancient Egypt's most famous neighborhood for the dead.

Which is exactly why it's been robbed over and over. Ancient thieves, medieval thieves, nineteenth-century "collectors" with shovels and no manners. If something shiny was buried at Saqqara, odds are somebody grabbed it a long time ago.

Now, the coffins date to roughly the 600s and 500s BC. Egyptologists call this the Late Period, and specifically the Twenty-Sixth Dynasty. And this era had a lot going on.

Just before it, Egypt had been a mess. Rival rulers split the country. Kings from Nubia, to the south, took over. Then the Assyrian Empire invaded and sacked cities. Then a local ruler named Psamtik the First stepped in, kicked the foreigners out, and pulled Egypt back together.

What followed was a kind of comeback era. Egypt got rich again. Temples got rebuilt. And people got really into the "good old days." Artists copied styles from pyramid times, almost two thousand years earlier. Think of it like a culture going through a massive retro phase, where everything old is cool again.

But the comeback had an expiration date. In 525 BC, the Persian Empire marched in and conquered Egypt. So the people in these coffins lived during a golden window, with danger on both sides of it.

## 3. THE STORY / DISCOVERY

The dig was run by an Egyptian team working for the country's Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities. And it didn't start with fifty-nine. It started small.

In September 2020, the team cleared a burial shaft and found thirteen coffins stacked inside. That alone was big news. Then they kept digging. More coffins turned up. Then another shaft. Then another. The count went to twenty-seven. Then fifty-nine. By the time the announcement was made in October, there were three shafts, each roughly thirty to forty feet deep, packed with wooden coffins.

And these weren't plain boxes. They were human-shaped coffins, the kind Egyptologists call anthropoid. Each one had a carved face on the lid. Painted eyes. Wigs. Collars. Columns of hieroglyphs running down the front. And after 2,500 years in the dark, a lot of the colors were still bright. Blues, yellows, reds, greens. Like they'd been painted last summer.

Alongside the coffins, the team found small statues and figurines, including statues of gods tied to death and rebirth. One standout was a gilded bronze statue of the god Nefertem, a god linked to the lotus flower, the sunrise and new beginnings. It was inlaid with precious stones and had his name written on the base.

Then came the moment that went viral. In front of reporters and cameras, the team opened one of the coffins. Inside lay a mummy, still wrapped in linen, still decorated, still exactly where someone had placed it before the Persians ever showed up.

But there were also some things missing. There was no golden treasure room. No famous king. No pharaoh's crown. This wasn't Tutankhamun. In some ways, it was something more interesting. These were ordinary people. Well, ordinary-ish. And they were stacked like a family storage unit.

So why were they stacked? Shafts like these were often reused. A family, or a group connected by the same job, would dig one deep shaft and keep adding burials over time. Instead of building a new tomb for every person, you used the same vertical "building" and added more residents. It was cheaper, and it was safer. A deep shaft packed with sand and rubble is a lot harder to rob than a fancy tomb with a door.

And that's likely the answer to one of our big questions. The thieves didn't find these coffins because the shafts were deep, filled in and unremarkable on the surface. Over the centuries, sand did the rest. Saqqara hid its own secret in plain sight.

## 4. FIRST REVEAL

But the biggest question is still hanging there. Who were these people?

For that, the team didn't need DNA or a time machine. They needed to read.

Those columns of hieroglyphs on the coffin lids weren't decoration. They were ID cards. Ancient Egyptian coffins usually carried the name of the person inside, plus their titles, their job, and often the names of their parents. And the inscriptions here pointed to the same answer again and again.

These were priests. And senior officials.

The officials announced that the coffins belonged to members of the religious and administrative elite of the Twenty-Sixth Dynasty. People who worked in temples and served in the state. Not kings, but the people who kept the kingdom running. Middle management with very nice coffins.

And honestly, the coffins themselves back that up. Good wood was expensive in Egypt. The country doesn't have big forests, so quality timber often had to be imported. Add skilled carvers, painters and scribes to write the spells, and a coffin like this was a serious investment. Not pharaoh money, but definitely "I have a good government job" money.

And that fits the location. These shafts sit in an area of Saqqara connected with a temple to Bastet, the cat goddess. This same area has produced mummified cats, cat statues and, in recent years, even mummified lion cubs. It was a sacred zone. So it makes sense that the people who served the gods would want to be buried close to them.

In other words, the mystery crowd in the shafts was basically the staff of a temple, being laid to rest right next to the office.

## 5. ZOOM-OUT TANGENT

Now, you might be wondering, why did the Egyptians care so much about writing names on everything?

Because for them, a name wasn't just a label. It was part of you.

The ancient Egyptians believed a person was made of several parts. There was your physical body. There was your ka, a kind of life force. There was your ba, often shown as a bird with a human head, which could fly in and out of the tomb. And there was your ren. Your name.

The ren was a big deal. If your name survived, you survived. If people kept saying it, writing it and reading it, you kept existing in the afterlife. If your name was erased, it was like being deleted.

The best modern comparison is probably your online account. Your username is how the system knows you exist. As long as the account is active, you're "in." But if someone deletes it, all your stuff vanishes. Egyptians were basically making sure their afterlife account never got deactivated. Writing their name on the coffin was like saving your password in five different places so you never get locked out.

That's also why the Egyptians had a nasty punishment for their worst enemies. They chiseled their names off monuments. That wasn't just petty. It was an attempt to erase someone from eternity. The female pharaoh Hatshepsut had many of her images and names attacked after her death. And the "heretic" king Akhenaten was later left off official king lists, as if he'd never ruled at all.

So for a priest or an official, writing their name on the coffin, again and again, was cheap insurance against being forgotten.

And here's the wild part. When those archaeologists read the names on the coffins at Saqqara and spoke them out loud to the world's cameras, they were doing exactly what these priests and officials had hoped for 2,500 years ago. They were saying their names again.

## CLOSING

So let's go back to the questions we started with.

Who was inside? Priests and officials of the Twenty-Sixth Dynasty, the people who ran Egypt's temples and offices during its last great comeback before the Persians arrived.

Why were they crammed together? Because deep, shared shafts were practical, cheaper and harder to rob. Ancient apartment buildings for the dead.

And how did the robbers miss them? The shafts were deep, sealed and buried under sand at a site so big that even three thousand years of thieves couldn't search every corner.

And the story didn't stop at fifty-nine. Just weeks later, the same team announced more than a hundred additional coffins from the same area. Saqqara, it turns out, still has plenty of residents nobody has met yet.

Which means somewhere out there, under the sand, there are more names. Waiting to be read.

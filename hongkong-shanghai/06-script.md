# How Did a Swamp Become the Richest City in Asia… Twice?

**Target:** 1,925 words (±5%) · **Final:** 1,885 words · ~10:32 at 2.98 wps

In 1841, Britain's foreign secretary looked at the island his navy had just seized off the coast of China and dismissed it as a barren island with hardly a house upon it.

A few years later, a group of British traders set up on a strip of mud beside a river further north. The ground was so soft that heavy buildings needed wide timber foundations just to stop them sinking.

The rock became Hong Kong.

The mud became Shanghai.

Between them, these two places have taken turns as China's richest, most important city for most of the last hundred and fifty years, and at their peaks, among the richest cities in Asia.

Neither had gold. Neither had oil. Neither had good farmland. One was a rock, the other was a swamp.

So how did they win, and why did they keep swapping places?

One of them lost its crown when an army marched in. One built a skyline on farmland in less than thirty years. And one port went from the busiest on Earth to outside the top ten.

The answer is a rule hiding on the map. Once you see it, it explains both cities, and it tells you which one is likely to win next.

We'll come back to it at the end.

Start with the river.

The Yangtze is the longest river in Asia, running about six thousand three hundred kilometers from the Tibetan Plateau to the sea.

Its basin holds roughly a third of China's population, and ocean-going ships can sail hundreds of kilometers inland along it.

All that water carries mud. Over thousands of years, the river dumped so much silt at its mouth that it built new land, pushing the coastline steadily east into the sea.

Shanghai sits on that land. Most of the city is only a few meters above sea level.

Whoever controls the mouth of the Yangtze controls the door to the richest river basin in China.

Now look south, about twelve hundred kilometers down the coast.

Hong Kong has almost no flat land. It's a cluster of steep, rocky islands and hills.

But between Hong Kong Island and the Kowloon peninsula lies something rare: a deep, sheltered natural harbor, protected from typhoons, big enough for a whole fleet.

One city has a river. The other has a harbor.

Round one: Shanghai.

In 1842, Britain won the First Opium War. The Treaty of Nanjing handed it Hong Kong Island outright, and forced China to open five ports to foreign trade.

One of them was Shanghai.

The war had been fought to keep British merchants selling opium into China. Some of the same trading firms that made fortunes from it would soon build the first offices in both cities.

British merchants were given land north of the old walled town, along a muddy riverbank. They built an embankment to hold back the water. They called it the Bund, from a Hindi word for embankment.

In 1863, the British and American areas merged into the International Settlement. The French ran their own concession next door.

Inside, foreigners lived under their own laws, their own police and their own courts. Chinese officials had little say.

Then disaster drove people in. The Taiping Rebellion, one of the deadliest wars of the nineteenth century, sent waves of refugees into the foreign settlements, the one place the fighting couldn't reach.

Land prices soared. The swamp started paying for itself.

Banks followed. In 1865, a new bank was founded in Hong Kong to finance trade along the China coast. A month later, it opened in Shanghai. Today it's known as HSBC.

Hong Kong grew too. In 1898, Britain leased the land north of Kowloon, the New Territories, for ninety-nine years. That deadline would shape the city's future a century later.

By the 1930s, Shanghai had more than three million people. It handled around half of China's foreign trade, and most of the foreign money invested in the country.

Its skyline of banks and trading houses along the Bund was the most famous in Asia. It had department stores, cinemas, jazz clubs and a racecourse in the middle of town. Foreigners called it the Paris of the East.

But the same openness that made Shanghai rich made it a target.

In 1937, Japan attacked the city. The battle lasted three months and devastated much of it.

Then, in May 1949, Communist troops marched into Shanghai.

And the money started running.

Round two: Hong Kong.

The new government took over private businesses, banks and factories. Foreign companies were squeezed out.

Many of Shanghai's industrialists, especially its textile mill owners, had already moved their machines and money south.

To the one piece of China still run by Britain.

Hong Kong's population exploded, from around six hundred thousand at the end of the war to over two million by 1950.

Then came a second shock. When the Korean War broke out, the United States and the United Nations restricted trade with China.

Hong Kong had lived as a trading port, moving goods in and out of China. Almost overnight, much of that trade dried up.

So it had to make things instead.

The Shanghai mill owners brought spinning machines and know-how. The refugees brought cheap labor. The harbor connected it all to the world.

Hong Kong started producing textiles, then plastic flowers, toys, wigs, watches and electronics.

One young factory owner, Li Ka-shing, made his first fortune from plastic flowers, then went on to become one of the richest people in Asia.

It kept taxes low, and stayed a free port with British law and courts. Money could come in and leave easily.

In the 1960s, its financial secretary, John Cowperthwaite, reportedly refused to collect detailed economic statistics, worried that officials would use them as an excuse to interfere.

By the 1990s, the average person in Hong Kong was richer than the average person in Britain, the country that still ruled it.

Meanwhile, Shanghai had stalled.

It remained China's industrial powerhouse, but under the planned economy, most of what it earned went to Beijing. By some estimates, for decades it supplied around a sixth of the central government's revenue.

Its old buildings were left to decay. For decades, very little new was built. The Bund went quiet.

In 1978, China began opening up. And suddenly, Hong Kong's location mattered more than ever.

Shenzhen, China's first Special Economic Zone, was right across the border.

Hong Kong factory owners moved their production into mainland China by the thousands, where land and labor were far cheaper. Hong Kong itself turned into the office: the banks, the lawyers, the shipping firms and the port.

For years, more than half of all foreign investment into China flowed through Hong Kong.

Its harbor became the busiest container port in the world.

But Beijing had not forgotten the river.

Round three: Shanghai, again.

In 1990, the government announced the Pudong New Area, on the east bank of the river, directly across from the Bund.

At the time, Pudong was mostly farmland, marsh and old warehouses. Locals had a saying: better a bed in Puxi, the old city, than a room in Pudong.

The same year, the Shanghai Stock Exchange reopened.

Then the building began.

The finance district, called Lujiazui, rose on a bend of the river directly facing the old banks of the Bund. The view across the water became the symbol of China's rise. Today, Pudong on its own has an economy bigger than many countries.

The Oriental Pearl Tower went up in 1994. The Jin Mao Tower in 1999. The Shanghai World Financial Center in 2008. And in 2015, the Shanghai Tower, at six hundred and thirty-two meters the second-tallest building in the world at the time.

On a former swamp, engineers had to drive foundations deep into the soft ground just to hold them up.

In 2004, Shanghai even opened the world's first commercial high-speed maglev line, running to the airport at over four hundred kilometers an hour.

Shanghai also solved its biggest weakness. The Yangtze mouth was too shallow for the largest container ships. So in 2005, it opened a deep-water port on islands out at sea, called Yangshan, linked to the mainland by a bridge over thirty kilometers long.

Since 2010, Shanghai has been the busiest container port on Earth. Today it handles close to fifty million containers a year.

Hong Kong, once number one, has fallen out of the top ten.

And the size of Shanghai's economy has overtaken Hong Kong's.

But here's the honest part.

Neither city got there for free.

Shanghai is sinking. Decades of pumping groundwater out of the soft ground caused parts of the city to drop by more than two meters during the twentieth century. With most of it barely above sea level, it now depends on sea walls and floodgates, and rising seas make that harder every year.

Hong Kong ran out of flat land long ago. A large share of its business district and homes sit on land reclaimed from the sea, and its housing is among the most expensive in the world.

And politics has caught up with both.

In 1997, Britain handed Hong Kong back to China. After the mass protests of 2019, Beijing imposed a national security law in 2020, and the line that once made Hong Kong different has blurred.

Shanghai showed the opposite risk in 2022, when a strict COVID lockdown shut most of its twenty-five million people indoors for about two months.

Hong Kong is still one of the world's biggest financial centers, and one of the top places on Earth for companies to list their shares.

Per person, Hong Kong is still much richer. The average resident produces around twice as much as the average person in Shanghai.

So, back to that rule hiding on the map.

Look at the pattern.

When China was forced open by foreign powers, Shanghai at the river mouth won.

When China closed its doors, the money ran to Hong Kong, the one door that stayed open.

When China opened again, Hong Kong won first, because it was right next to the opening.

And when Beijing chose to rebuild its own door at the mouth of its biggest river, Shanghai won again.

The rule is simple: the city that wins is whichever one happens to be China's open door.

Not the one with the best land. The one with the best position, at the moment the door swings open.

So, the scorecard.

Geography: Shanghai has the river. Hong Kong has the harbor. Both had terrible land.

Resources: none to speak of. Just trade, money and people passing through.

History: both were shaped by war, empire and revolution, and both took in waves of refugees.

Decisions: Hong Kong chose low taxes, free trade and foreign law. Shanghai was chosen by Beijing, twice.

A rock and a swamp became two of the richest cities in Asia, trading places as China opened and closed. Not because of what was in the ground. Because of where the ground was.
